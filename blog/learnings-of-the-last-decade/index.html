Learnings of the Last Decade

A Tale of Building Thriving Teams

Originally published on Substack, April 11, 2026.

At a 2024 innovation summit hosted by Procter & Gamble (Signal P&G), I heard Rebekah Homkes describe how organizations navigate uncertainty through three stages: Survive → Reset → Thrive. In simple terms: When the environment changes, organizations must first stabilize, then redesign themselves for the new reality, and only then can they turn to thriving. This resonated deeply with what I've observed firsthand.

The Trap of Success in Stable Environments

In stable environments, organizations often succeed through: Clear hierarchy and centralized decision-making, standardized processes, tight hyper-specialists role alignment. There's nothing inherently wrong with this. In fact, it works.

The problem emerges when the environment changes but the structure doesn't. Organizations expect rigid systems to suddenly become agile, adaptive, and resilient. But adaptability is not a switch you just turn on; it's more like a muscle. And if it hasn't been exercised, it atrophies.

Thriving Starts with the Leader

To build a thriving organization, especially one that's facing constant change, transformation must start with leadership. Organizational transformation requires personal transformation. And at the core is resilience.

In my experience, the most resilient leaders tend to come from one of two paths: those grounded in strong self-worth (often rooted in secure early environments) and those who have faced adversity and grown through it. Both arrive at the same place: inner stability and comfort in external uncertainty.

Pain as a Signal, Not an Obstacle

I learned this lesson the hard way. Insights from resilience practitioners like Cindy Finch reinforce a simple truth: pain is not something to avoid, it's something to re-signify. During my most recent reinvention, I ignored clear signals of physical stress, emotional discomfort, and lack of psychological safety of a new environment. Instead, I rationalized staying for far too long. Looking back, fear was not something to suppress; it was information. Fear often signals something unseen that needs to be faced. I had the priorities reversed: I listened to logic before listening to my own body. That was the mistake.

Clarity Creates Energy

Leaders with clarity don't just execute; they energize. As coach, friend and supporter of my work, Rob Craven taught me: when personal purpose aligns with business purpose, energy becomes contagious. In one of my most meaningful team experiences, the brand purpose aligned with my personal purpose at about 60%; however, my team environment aligned with it at a 100%. I define that period as my Ikigai moment. Many of my readers already know that my personal purpose in life is "to feel the world is a playground" and my leadership role is building that playground within teams that in turn makes resilient high-performing teams. What did that look like in practice? Zero internal drama, high trust and mutual care, freedom to experiment and fail forward, and strong ownership of ideas. When individuals feel safe, they take risks, focus on what's meaningful work for them, and support each other. And then something powerful happens. The care for your team translates into care for the consumer, which in turn translates into brand love.

Know Individuals for a Strong Team Culture

High-performing teams are not built through abstraction; they are built through deep understanding of individuals. Inspired by principles like "Individualization," we invested in monthly personal spotlights – team members sharing about their personal lives – and tools like Cloverleaf to understand each others' working styles. This created care, context, distributed decision-making, and strong collaboration.

Interestingly, I believe that older, private, family-run businesses intuitively understood this. They hired for values alignment, not just capability. There's wisdom there and maybe we should look at older practices and extract it from them.

Creating Your Own Luck

In Earning Serendipity, Glenn Llopis outlines a model to create your own luck: See Opportunities → Sow Opportunities → Grow Opportunities → Share Opportunities. My addition: when you aim for something bigger than yourself and work on readiness, you increase your chances for luck. Life in turn conspires for your success.

The Cost of Losing Yourself

My most recent learning is also maybe one of the simplest. I compromised on a core value of mine, love over fear. When I started to conform to external expectations, prioritized following guidelines and drifted from core values, I entered what I call career quicksand. A moment in which you are no longer leading and start following something you don't even believe in. It doesn't feel like failure; it feels like slow misalignment.

Thriving Is an Inside-Out Process

Thriving organizations are not built through structure alone. They are built through leaders with high self-awareness, resilience, and purpose. And above all is the courage to choose growth over comfort again and again. This is also known as conviction.
