You Can't Transform an Organization Without Transforming Yourself

Most leaders try to fix behavior – few understand what's actually driving it

Originally published on Substack, April 19, 2026.

I talk a lot about organizational transformation: new strategies, new structures, and new systems. But less often I talk about the one thing that makes all of it work: the human being leading it.

Because I strongly believe that transformation doesn't start at the organizational level; it starts at the personal level. I believe more integrated human beings make better leaders; not perfect leaders, integrated ones. And integration is not something you either have or don't have; it's a skill. One that can be developed over time, with intention.

Recently, I visited a friend who told me something that stuck with me "You're one of the most self-aware people I know." I didn't take that as a compliment; I took it as a question. Is that actually true? And if it is – what does self-awareness even look like in practice? This piece is my attempt to answer that. Both as a reflection on my own journey, and as a framework you can use for yours.

The Question Most People Avoid: Who am I?

It sounds simple, but it's not. As adults, we rarely ask it seriously. And when we do, we tend to answer it with surface-level labels: What we do (roles), what we've achieved (markers), what others see (external perceptions). But those are just the tip of the iceberg.

The Iceberg (The Framework)

Who you are operates at multiple levels. What people see is only the surface. Underneath, there's a much deeper structure driving everything.

From the bottom up, mine looks like this: Self-Worth → Need for Control → Belief for Belonging → Characteristic Attitude → Capable Behavior

In the US, there's a big tendency for people to spend their lives trying to fix behavior. Even therapy strategies like DBT (dialectical behavior therapy) or CBT (cognitive behavioral therapy) are just examples of this tendency. Few go deep enough to understand what's driving it.

My Iceberg (Unfiltered)

I'll share mine – not because it's right, but because it's real. And with this I hope you take a stab at yours. Let's start from the bottom up:

  • Self-Worth: I grew up feeling a form of rejection. Not from bad intentions – but from an environment I interpreted as inconsistent and emotionally absent at times. To me, it felt like love was conditional. That interpretation became foundational.
  • Need for Control: From there, I developed a quiet and unintentional internal framework: If I rely on myself, I stay safe. I became "self-made" psychologically – not as an external identity, but as an internal survival mechanism.
  • Belief for Belonging: At the same time, I internalized something else: if people like me, they won't reject me. So I adapted, I became agreeable, attuned, and a people-pleaser. Not as a personality trait, but as a survival strategy.
  • Characteristic Attitude: From there, I built an identity: being helpful, adaptable, and useful to others. Because if you're useful, you're needed; and if you're needed, you're less likely to be rejected.
  • Capable Behavior: And finally, the part the external world rewards: productivity, discipline, preparation, and relentlessness. From the outside, it looks like high performance. From the inside, it can feel like it's never enough.

Why This Matters

We don't see the world as it is, we see it through the lenses we've built over time. These are mine, which means I don't just carry these patterns internally – I project them outward. I notice them in others. I have to admit that I sometimes judge them in others. And more often than not, what we judge in others is something we don't allow ourselves to be.

The Work I'm Doing Now

I still have a lot of work to do. One area I'm focusing on intentionally is reconnecting with my body and emotions. My default mode is external: doing, thinking, and producing. Not feeling. So for me, growth looks like the opposite of what I've been rewarded for: slowing down, sitting still, paying attention. Transcendental meditation has become less of a practice and more of a necessity.

Where This Connects to Leadership

Here's the point: If you don't understand your iceberg, it will run your leadership. Your fears will shape your decisions. Your need for control will shape your team. Your need for belonging will shape your culture. And you won't even realize it, but the people around you see it. That's why personal transformation isn't optional for leaders; it's foundational.

Many of my readers have already heard of my personal purpose: to make you feel the world is a playground for all. In my leadership style, this means creating an environment for my team that's both safe and open – psychologically safe and open to play or create. I feel like the more you know about yourself, the more able you are to choose to act from your conscious mind versus your unconscious framework. When I'm conscious I'm running on love (building something worth aiming for), when I'm unconscious I'm running on fear of all the things I mentioned before – being in control, being liked, feeling useful and productive. Knowing myself and knowing my purpose gives me direction, a north star.
