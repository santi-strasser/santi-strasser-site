The Small Giant in Our Backyard

What a 55-year-old Minnesota hinge factory taught us about keeping purpose at the front of a business.

Written by Santi Strasser. Inspired by Ipalibo Da-Wariboko. Originally published on Substack, Sep 5, 2026.

A few weeks ago, Ipalibo and I set out chasing a question: How do companies keep purpose at the front of the business? Not purpose in the founding story. Not purpose on the wall by the elevators. Purpose alive in the decisions a company makes on an ordinary Tuesday.

We came at it from opposite ends. Ipalibo has spent his career as a brand strategist, sitting with global companies while they decide what they stand for, then watching what happens when that position meets a P&L. I've watched him retrofit purpose into brands that had been gutted years before he ever walked in the room. I've spent my career on the other side of the spectrum, inside small teams, doing things "no one else is doing."

We assumed we'd land on opposite sides of the debate. Instead, the question sent us to a book, and the book sent me somewhere I did not expect. The book is Small Giants: Companies That Choose to Be Great Instead of Big, by Bo Burlingham, a longtime editor at Inc.

The premise fits in a sentence and it sat with me for weeks. Burlingham profiles fourteen privately held American companies that were handed the thing every business is supposed to want, growth, scale, acquisition, the next market, and turned it down on purpose. Among them: Anchor Brewing, Zingerman's, Clif Bar, Righteous Babe Records, Union Square Hospitality Group, O.C. Tanner, and a hinge manufacturer outside of the Twin Cities named Reell Precision Manufacturing.

None of them were anti-growth; they grew. What they refused was growth as the default answer. Burlingham calls the thing they share "mojo," an unfashionable word for something real. These companies are rooted in a specific place and a specific community. They keep unusually intimate relationships with customers and suppliers. Their governance structures are all over the map, almost none of them conventional. And in every case, someone in charge made a conscious, articulated decision that the point of the company was something other than getting as big as possible, as fast as possible.

Here is the part that stayed with me. Purpose didn't survive in these companies because it was well written. It survived because the company kept saying no to things that would have paid off. Purpose is not a statement; it is a running tally of declined opportunities.

Chapter 6

Burlingham calls it "Galt's Gulch." It's about Reell Precision Manufacturing, founded in St. Paul in 1970 and now in Vadnais Heights, just north of the city. Three engineers who left 3M together: Bob Wahlstedt, Dale Merrick, and Lee Johnson. They make wrap spring clutches and torque hinges.

Three things about how they ran it:

  • For most of its history there was no CEO. The three founders led together as co-equals, and major decisions required unanimous agreement. All three, or it didn't happen. By 2002 the company had co-CEOs. It has had a single chief executive since 2009.
  • They wrote down what the company was for, in language most boards would sand off within a week: "Reell is a team united in the operation of a business based on the practical application of spiritual values..."
  • They deliberately dismantled their own authority. Reell moved from what they called Command-Direct-Control to Teach-Equip-Trust. The operating principle was direction, teaching, and persuasion, not orders. Decisions got pushed down to the lowest level where someone could competently make them. Trust before control; love over fear, basically, thirty years before I'd put a name to it.

In the fall of 2002, Reell published a piece called "A Matter of Conscience." An engineer named Joe Arnold had been asked to design a hinge for a display box for a cigarette company. Good work, well within his skill, and Reell needed the revenue. The company was in a hard financial stretch and sales wanted the business.

Arnold said he couldn't do it. He couldn't reconcile building something that would help sell an addictive product, particularly with kids in mind. He said so, out loud, to sales and to the co-CEOs.

Reell didn't take the contract.

Sit with that for a second. A single engineer, not a founder, not an executive, publicly objected to a revenue-generating project on the grounds of conscience, during a downturn. The company treated it as legitimate input instead of insubordination, and then it paid for the objection out of its own revenue at the worst possible moment to be paying for anything.

That doesn't happen by accident. It doesn't happen because of a values statement on a wall. It happens because someone spent thirty years building an organization where saying that out loud was survivable, and where being right about it was expensive.

It wasn't the only time. In late 2000, Reell's revenue dropped roughly 35%. Leadership had two real options: lay off about 20% of the workforce, forty-plus people, or cut pay across the board by 12 to 15%. In February 2001, they took the pay cut. One employee left. Morale went up.

Why I couldn't put the book down

For three and a half years, I co-founded and grew a venture inside a publicly traded Fortune 500 company. Three of us at the start. One owned the technical side, research and development, supply chain. Another owned sales, ops, and finance. I owned marketing, insights, and brand experience. We ran it flat; three co-founders, one team, and a working agreement that we moved together or we didn't move – disagree and commit.

As the venture grew, the same question kept coming back from investors: is there any example of a tri-leader structure that's actually worked at scale? We never had a good answer. We had conviction, and conviction is not evidence, not in a room where you're asking for several million dollars at a time.

Then I read Chapter 6. The answer had been sitting a short drive from our office the entire time. A fifty-five-year-old precision manufacturer run by three people who had to agree unanimously, that had made it through multiple recessions without a layoff and had its ethics studied by business school faculty. I couldn't believe it. We'd spent years being asked for proof, and the case study was in our backyard.

Reell wasn't just admired after the fact. University of St. Thomas' Kenneth Goodpaster, who held the David and Barbara Koch Endowed Chair in Business Ethics at the Opus College of Business there starting in 1990, wrote a teaching case on Reell, and in 2015 published Respect in Action: Applying Subsidiarity in Business. Quick note: as a Gopher myself, I'd have wished someone from the UMN had written it - I digress.

Subsidiarity is the idea underneath everything Reell did. Decisions belong at the lowest level where they can be made competently. Authority moves down until it reaches the person closest to the work, and it stays there. I call it distributed decision-making in which leadership's job isn't to make the call, it's to make sure the person making the call is equipped to make it.

That's the difference between a company that talks about empowerment and a company that's structurally incapable of taking it back. Reell's tri-leadership wasn't a quirk of three friends who liked each other; it was a mechanism. Requiring unanimity among three leaders makes it procedurally impossible for any one person to override the company's stated values on a bad day.

That is what it looks like when purpose stops being a belief and becomes a resilient architecture.

What I actually found

Reell's unanimity requirement was a rule. Ours was an agreement; three co-founders with equal say and every intention of keeping it, but no charter, no shares, and no governing body anyone outside the room could point to. Our venture was stage-gate funded on approval by a board of executives who weren't shareholders, hadn't put in their own capital, and could not be voted out.

That board was the whole point. Its job was to hold a line between us and the part of the company that runs on quarterly numbers, long enough for something new to become real. For three and a half years it worked. We ran it flat, and the venture got to a double-digit-million run rate doing something the rest of the business had no mechanism to attempt on its own timeline.

Then the structure was dissolved and the venture was folded into the part of the company it had been shielded from. Nobody voted us out. There was no extraordinary general meeting, no press release, no threshold anyone had to clear. Our structure could be replaced by an org chart, and it was, in an afternoon. Nobody broke a rule. There was no rule to break to begin with.

So what I found in Chapter 6 wasn't proof that our model worked. It was a description of what our model was missing. A charter is a promise enforced by someone whose whole legal existence depends on enforcing it. What we had was a program, generously funded and genuinely meant, and a program is a promise that lasts exactly as long as its main sponsor wants to keep it – when that sponsor leaves, it's up to the next person to decide.

What makes a governance structure protect judgment isn't that someone designed it. It's that someone made it expensive to undo.

And then Reell nearly fell apart

I want to include this next part, because leaving it out would make for a nicer story and a less useful one.

In February 2008, Inc. published a piece about Reell titled "Paradise Lost." It was written by Bo Burlingham, two years after Small Giants came out. The author who put Reell on the list went back and documented it coming apart.

The company that had never laid anyone off was losing money. It was tangled in accusations and lawsuits. Its internal debate over policy had turned bitter and personal. The company famous for its culture was posting the highest turnover and the lowest morale in its history. The recession that followed brought Reell close to failure. It survived, under new leadership. It's still there today. Still employee-owned.

I don't think this invalidates anything in Chapter 6. I think it's the most important fact in the whole article.

Purpose is not a state you achieve. Reell didn't "have" purpose in 2001 and lose it later. Purpose is a practice that has to be re-earned by every generation of leadership, under every new set of conditions, and it degrades the moment people assume it's self-sustaining. The pay cut in 2001 didn't immunize them against 2008. Nothing does.

Which is, honestly, the answer to the question Ipalibo and I started with. There's no mechanism that keeps purpose at the front of the business permanently. There's only the discipline of putting it back there, over and over, usually at cost.

I can't say I've built a small giant. What I can say is I got a taste of it. I got to help scale a high-performing, purpose-driven team that genuinely felt like a playground, a place safe enough to try something in public and fail at it, inside a publicly traded Fortune 500 company. I know what that felt like from the inside. I know what it costs (deeply and personally), especially when what you've built starts to threaten the larger bureaucracy around it.

And I know now what we didn't have. Purpose stayed at the front of Reell's business for three decades because three people made it structurally impossible to quietly abandon, and it slipped the moment that structure stopped being tended. Ours was never structurally impossible to abandon. It just hadn't been abandoned yet.

Build the architecture. Then keep taking care of it. And before you assume you've already got one, ask the harder question: not whose decisions your company protects right now, but how hard it would actually be for someone to undo that protection.
